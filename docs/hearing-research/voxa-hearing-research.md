# Teach Voxa to hear only the user

Voxa can beat Alexa, Siri and Google Assistant at "hear every word, never hear itself" because it has the one thing every vendor patent works hard to approximate: **the exact waveform, text and per-word timing of everything it is about to say**. The big assistants all put barge-in detection after a reference-driven echo canceller. Amazon then classifies wake words as self-playback or user speech from AEC statistics such as ERLE and reference correlation ([US10586534B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10586534)), and feeds the playback reference into its detector networks ([arXiv 2111.10639](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.10639)). Even so, commercial systems still trade speed against false stops. GPT-4o stops in about 0.23 s but responds to background speech in 93% of trials, while Amazon Nova Sonic ignores 99% of background speech but takes 2.25 s to stop ([Full-Duplex-Bench v1.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.23159v1)). The winning design for Voxa is layered, and every layer exists in open, CPU-only form. First, an exact, timestamped TTS reference replaces the noisy default-sink monitor. Second, WebRTC AEC3 replaces the hand-written NLMS; it is now pip-installable as `pywebrtc-audio` ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/pywebrtc-audio/)). Third, a reference-driven residual suppressor removes the nonlinear echo cheap speakers produce. Fourth, an echo-aware "suspect then confirm" barge-in duck answers in about 200 ms and commits only after words arrive. Fifth, a word-timed own-speech filter, an optional speaker check against an "anti-enrolled" TTS voice, and semantic endpointing with Smart Turn finish the job ([pipecat-ai/smart-turn](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/smart-turn)). None of this should be tuned by ear. **The first deliverable is an offline test bench** that renders Voxa's own TTS through simulated and recorded echo paths, mixes in labelled user speech, runs the real pipeline, and emits ASR-level gates: zero self-interruptions on the smoke suite, at most 1% of TTS-only clips producing any transcript, at least 97% user word recall, and at most 3 dB near-end attenuation in double talk. The roadmap at the end orders eighteen small tasks (five bench tasks first, then thirteen improvements), each with a numeric pass gate a local coding model can run.

**How to read this report.** A sentence with an inline link states a cited fact from the research notes. Paragraphs or sentences that begin **Inference** are my reasoning or design proposals and are not published results. Statements marked **Observed in Voxa** come from reading the Voxa source tree (`/home/rhy/strata-fleet/repos/voxa`) during this synthesis. Gate values in the roadmap are proposals unless a source is linked; calibrate them against the first baseline run.

## Every big assistant gates barge-in behind the echo canceller, and still trades speed for false stops

The public record is consistent across vendors: nobody trusts a microphone VAD while the device is talking. Amazon's patent US10586534B1 (filed 2017, granted 2020) sends the loudspeaker reference to the AEC and judges each wake-word detection by AEC statistics over the detection window. **Steady-state ERLE is typically 15–25 dB during playback and about 0 dB with no playback.** Steady ERLE plus high reference-to-mic correlation means the wake word came from playback and is rejected. A sharp ERLE drop or correlation collapse means double talk, so the wake word is accepted and playback pauses or ducks. The patent adds hold-time logic, a high-ERLE threshold, and an ERLE-based bypass so near-end commands are not wrongly rejected ([US10586534B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10586534)). Amazon's later "implicit AEC" work goes further and feeds the playback reference as an extra input channel to the keyword spotter and the device-directed-speech detector. That **cut device-directed false rejects during playback by 56% relative** on real Alexa data, and matched a neural-AEC-plus-KWS cascade at about 10x less compute ([arXiv 2111.10639](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.10639)). An earlier Amazon paper trained the wake-word model on audio mixed with music and TV at varying signal-to-interference ratios to absorb residual echo, cutting false rejects 30–45% relative ([arXiv 1808.00563](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.00563)).

Apple's HomePod write-up is the most candid about why the linear canceller is never enough. **Echo can be 30–40 dB louder than far-field speech**, and after multichannel linear AEC the residual is still 10–20 dB above the speech at loud volume because of nonlinear coupling. Apple therefore runs a DNN trained on real HomePod echo recordings to predict a speech-presence mask, which drives a Wiener filter and a suppression gain. The "Hey Siri" detector then picks the best of several separated streams. Apple reports **about 90% relative WER improvement in the playback condition**, with the whole front end using under 15% of one A8 core ([Apple ML Research](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/optimizing-siri-on-homepod-in-far-field-settings)). Google's published path is neural AEC tuned for ASR: a waveform Conformer AEC ([arXiv 2205.03481](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.03481)), a patent application stacking reference features with mic features for joint AEC, enhancement and separation ([US20230038982](https://patents.justia.com/patent/20230038982)), and a 1.6M-parameter residual echo suppressor after linear AEC that **cut WER 38–53% beyond linear AEC alone**, with the authors stressing that TTS-like playback is the worst case ([Interspeech 2023](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2023/panchapagesan23_interspeech.pdf)). Sonos targets very low speech-to-music ratios with a decorrelating multichannel STFT AEC ([Sonos tech blog](https://tech-blog.sonos.com/posts/sonos-at-icassp-2021/)). It also patented a second canceller for the device's own acknowledgement tone, which the converged music AEC would otherwise cancel ([US10621981](https://uspto.report/patent/grant/10621981)). Microsoft's DeepVQE does joint AEC, noise suppression and dereverberation in Teams, and replaces DSP delay estimation with cross-attention alignment ([arXiv 2306.03177](https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/2306.03177); [arXiv 2208.11308](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.11308.pdf)).

Self-wake from other media is handled differently. Amazon fingerprints known ads (Haitsma–Kalker style sign bits compared by bit-error rate) against a small on-device set and a large cloud set, and treats a match across at least three concurrent customers as a live "media event" ([Amazon Science](https://amazon.science/blog/why-alexa-wont-wake-up-when-she-hears-her-name-in-amazons-super-bowl-ad)). Press coverage puts the effect at **80–90% of devices suppressed** for broadcast "Alexa" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2019/01/31/heres-why-amazons-super-bowl-ad-wont-trigger-alexa/)). Google's patent family covers server-side fingerprint suppression ([US10867600B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10867600)) and inaudible watermarks in media ([US11244674](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11244674); [US11257498](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11257498)), plus suppressing commands aimed at another assistant ([US11972766B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11972766)).

After the trigger, every vendor runs a second-stage "is this meant for me?" check over the whole utterance. Apple's Siri pipeline has three parts: a latticeRNN using ASR-hypothesis competition, a streaming acoustic transformer on prosody and SNR, and a BERT-like text classifier that catches non-vocative mentions of "Siri" ([Apple ML Research](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/voice-trigger)). Apple has since moved to LLM-based multi-signal device-directed speech detection ([Apple ML Research](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/llm-device-directed-speech-detection)). Alexa's Follow-Up and Conversation Modes use acoustic and visual directedness ([Amazon Science](https://www.amazon.science/blog/change-to-alexa-wake-word-process-adds-natural-turn-taking)). Google's Look and Talk uses on-device gaze, Face Match and Voice Match before on-device intent checks ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/look-and-talk-natural-conversations-with-google-assistant/)). Alexa's "contextual barge-in" also passes the interruption's offset into the TTS to the dialogue manager, so "that one" resolves to what the user had actually heard ([Amazon Science](https://www.amazon.science/blog/change-to-alexa-wake-word-process-adds-natural-turn-taking)).

The LLM voice APIs are the cautionary tale. OpenAI Realtime's `server_vad` defaults to threshold 0.5, 300 ms prefix padding and 500 ms silence, and `interrupt_response=true` cancels the reply on any VAD speech start ([OpenAI API reference](https://developers.openai.com/docs/api-reference/realtime-sessions); [OpenAI VAD guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-vad.md)). The client must send `conversation.item.truncate` with `audio_end_ms`, the position actually played, so the transcript drops what the user never heard ([hexdocs](https://hexdocs.pm/ex_openai/2.0.0-beta/ExOpenAI.Components.RealtimeClientEventConversationItemTruncate.html)). Gemini Live likewise cancels and discards generation on an interruption, sends `interrupted=true`, and makes the client flush queued audio, with a server end-of-speech silence of about 800 ms ([AI Studio docs](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/live-api/capabilities)). Neither documents any echo removal, so a desktop client with poor AEC cuts itself off on its own voice.

How good are they really? Full-Duplex-Bench v1.5 measured overlap handling directly, and the numbers show a hard trade-off rather than a solved problem ([Full-Duplex-Bench v1.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.23159v1)):

| System | Responds to real interruption | Resumes after a backchannel | Responds to background speech | Time to stop |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-4o | 0.78 | 0.70 | 0.93 | 0.23 s |
| Gemini | 0.33 | 0.93 | 0.70 | 2.20 s |
| Amazon Nova Sonic | 0.24 | 0.98 | 0.01 | 2.25 s |
| Freeze-Omni | 0.72 | 0.80 | 0.63 | 1.42 s |
| Moshi | 0.50 | 0.06 | 0.21 | 1.16 s |

**Inference.** "Better than Google, Siri and Alexa" is concretely measurable. Voxa should stop audio within about 0.3 s, as GPT-4o does, while resuming after backchannels and ignoring background speech as Nova Sonic does. A fast duck followed by a slower commit is how to get both. Voxa has three structural advantages no vendor publication enjoys. It plays one known synthetic voice. It knows the text and word timings of every utterance, since `speech.py` already requests Edge TTS word boundaries (**Observed in Voxa**). And it usually serves one user. Its disadvantages are equally concrete: a single mic, cheap nonlinear laptop speakers, no custom-trained reference-conditioned model, and clocks it does not control. The roadmap spends Voxa's advantages to cover those weaknesses.

## A linear canceller removes 15–25 dB; the residual decides whether Voxa hears itself

WebRTC AEC3 is the practical state of the art for a CPU-only linear-plus-suppressor canceller, and it is designed for exactly Voxa's failure modes. It runs two partitioned frequency-domain filters, a "refined" main filter and a "coarse" shadow filter, and crossfades to the coarse output when the refined filter diverges (about a 10% power margin) ([echo_remover.cc](https://webrtc.googlesource.com/src/+/main/modules/audio_processing/aec3/echo_remover.cc)). After a coefficient copy it blocks refined-filter speed-ups for 100 ms to avoid divergence ([commit 992a96f](https://webrtc.googlesource.com/src/+/992a96f68e4cadc69ff85ffe85500010e761e2bd)). Its default configuration sets 13-block filters, leakage of 0.00005 when converged and 0.05 when diverged, a delay estimator of 5 matched filters on 4x-decimated signals, `echo_can_saturate=true`, and comfort noise. The residual suppressor has separate "normal" and much more transparent "nearend" tunings (`mask_lf` enr_transparent 0.3 vs 1.09), so it suppresses hard on echo and backs off during double talk ([EchoCanceller3Config](https://webrtc.googlesource.com/src/+/main/api/audio/echo_canceller3_config.h)). AEC3 detects clock drift from its delay history (`ClockdriftDetector`) and becomes more conservative; `echo_removal_control.has_clock_drift` forces that mode ([clockdrift_detector.h](https://webrtc.googlesource.com/src/+/53a7bb255dc00f7ef3f782ce99a3fcc2f4338726/modules/audio_processing/aec3/clockdrift_detector.h)). Mozilla filed a bug to set the flag whenever input and output devices differ ([Mozilla bug 1887774](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1887774)). Since September 2026, **`pywebrtc-audio` 0.2.0 ships Apache-2.0 wheels for Linux x86_64/aarch64 and Python 3.10–3.14 exposing an AEC3 `EchoCanceller`** at 16/32/48 kHz in 10 ms frames. Its `stream_delay_ms` is a hint that can be left at 0 for the internal estimator ([PyPI pywebrtc-audio](https://pypi.org/project/pywebrtc-audio/); [Strands docs](https://strandsagents.com/docs/labs/pywebrtc-audio/)). The notes did not verify whether it exposes the full config, including `has_clock_drift`.

SpeexDSP's MDF canceller is the lightweight alternative, and practitioners consistently report it falls apart when capture and playback clocks differ. Speex "is known for not resyncing smoothly" while WebRTC copes ([Launchpad bug 1261666](https://bugs.launchpad.net/ubuntu/+source/pulseaudio/+bug/1261666/comments/5)). It is acceptable on a single USB card with one shared clock ([Mycroft forum](https://community.mycroft.ai/t/fighting-with-pulseaudio-aec/9247)). **Inference.** Voxa's current canceller is in Speex's class. **Observed in Voxa:** `anc.py` is a 1024-tap NLMS (64 ms at 16 kHz), μ 0.5, with a 10 ms block, a freeze-on-double-talk rule, a "never worse" guard and a 300 ms cross-correlation delay search. Moving to Speex would gain nothing; moving to AEC3 gains a dual filter, a better delay tracker, drift handling and an ERLE-driven suppressor in one step.

Clock drift is not a corner case. A 100 ppm mismatch accumulates 360 ms of offset per hour ([Stack Overflow archive](https://kiwix.lema.org/stackoverflow.com_en_all_2023-11/questions/15727333/how-to-calculate-sound-card-clock-drift)). Only synchronous USB devices lock to the host clock ([ASIO4ALL](https://asio4all.org/device-aggregation/)), and patents note AEC works best within a few ppm ([US7680285B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7680285)). A 2025 paper estimated sample-rate offset with a coherence-drift plus GCC method from two previous 0.512 s segments and resampled the far-end reference. That **restored no-drift AEC performance up to about ±75 ppm**, although estimation from the AEC error signal faltered in double talk ([arXiv 2507.05399](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.05399v1)). **Inference.** Voxa's TTS turns last seconds, and 100 ppm × 5 s is only about 8 samples at 16 kHz. Re-estimating delay at every TTS onset and letting AEC3 track within the utterance is enough; a full adaptive resampler is only worth building if the bench shows long monologues degrading.

Nonlinear echo from small, cheap speakers is where single-mic systems lose. The notes found no recent primary evidence that explicit Volterra or Hammerstein models pay off. The industry pattern is a linear filter followed by a reference-driven residual suppressor, whether AEC3's saturation-aware suppressor or a neural postfilter ([EchoCanceller3Config](https://webrtc.googlesource.com/src/+/main/api/audio/echo_canceller3_config.h)). A 2024 hybrid of linear AEC plus a Bark-scale neural postfilter matched DeepVQE-S on the ICASSP 2023 blind set at about 10% of its compute ([arXiv 2404.11621](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.11621)). The neural landscape is summarised below.

| System | Type | Size | CPU cost | Open? | Notable result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ICASSP 2023 winner | hybrid | 1.5M | RTF 0.28 | no | score 0.854 ([arXiv 2309.12553](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.12553v1)) |
| DeepVQE | end-to-end | 7.5M | 3.66 ms/frame i7 | no weights | ties winner, 0.854 ([ar5iv 2306.03177](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.03177)) |
| DeepVQE-S | end-to-end | 0.59M | RTF 0.014 | no weights | DT echo MOS 4.00 vs 4.29 |
| LocalVQE v1.1 | pruned DeepVQE in GGML | 1.29M (~5 MB) | 3.40 ms per 16 ms hop, 1 thread | Apache-2.0, C lib + ctypes | blind ERLE 44.7 dB far-end single talk ([HF LocalVQE](https://huggingface.co/Trondtempia/LocalVQE)) |
| NKF | neural Kalman linear stage | 5.3K | RTF 0.09 | code on GitHub | robust adaptation with no DTD ([arXiv 2207.11388](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.11388v2)) |
| DTLN-aec | end-to-end | 1.8–10.4M | TF-lite | MIT | 2021 challenge top-5 ([arXiv 2010.14337](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14337)) |

LocalVQE's far-end single-talk AECMOS echo score (2.98) sits oddly against its 44.7 dB ERLE, and the source does not explain the gap ([HF LocalVQE](https://huggingface.co/Trondtempia/LocalVQE)). Treat it as a candidate to measure, not a known winner. Double talk is handled in modern systems without hard Geigel-style detectors: AEC3 uses shadow/main filters plus a near-end suppressor tuning, and NKF learns the Kalman gain ([arXiv 2207.11388](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.11388v2)).

**Inference.** The right stack for Voxa has four layers. The first is AEC3 fed with Voxa's exact TTS samples. The second is Voxa's existing per-band `refmask.py` (or LocalVQE as an experiment) applied to AEC3's output during TTS and for about 300 ms after, to cover the room tail. The third is an echo-aware detector gate. The fourth is keeping TTS output peaks at about −3 to −6 dBFS so the speaker is not driven into compression. Because Voxa knows its own reference exactly, the residual mask may be aggressive while TTS plays; the only thing it must protect is near-end speech.

## Voxa's exact TTS waveform is a reference no TV ad can match

The strongest published gains come from using the playback reference *beyond* the linear canceller, and they are largest when the playback is speech. Google's residual suppressor (1.6M params, 4 causal Conformer layers, trained partly on re-recorded internal TTS) gave the 38–53% WER cut already cited ([Interspeech 2023](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2023/panchapagesan23_interspeech.pdf)). NCSOFT's iPhonMatchNet added a reference input to a 656K-parameter keyword spotter for +0.13% parameters. It **cut self-playback false alarms from 73.97% to 0.15%** (mean absolute error) on Speech Commands, while an NLMS AEC baseline gave no significant gain in the same self-referencing case ([arXiv 2309.06096](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.06096v3)). Amazon cautions that reference-aware classifiers can learn quirks of the interferer, and that TTS-text-based approaches work only for TTS playback, not music ([arXiv 2111.09935](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.09935.pdf)). For Voxa's main problem, its own voice, that limitation does not bite. No open pretrained reference-conditioned residual suppressor or echo-aware ASR was found, so Voxa's practical reference-aware layer is signal processing plus small models it can calibrate itself.

Speaker identity is the second lever. Personal VAD classifies each frame as non-speech, target speech or non-target speech, with only 130K parameters in v1 ([arXiv 1908.04284](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.04284v4)). Personal VAD 2.0 reaches 1.0 MB int8 and 9.58 MFLOPs, and cut multi-speaker Concat WER from 41.0% to 27.2%. Its zero-embedding training keeps standard-VAD WER when nobody is enrolled (7.0% vs 6.9%) ([arXiv 2204.03793](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.03793v3)). Google released no code. FireRedChat released a streaming ECAPA-conditioned personalized VAD built for barge-in control, and reports a **10.2% false barge-in rate at 170 ms T90**, against 33.4% for LiveKit and 78.1% for TEN ([arXiv 2509.06502](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.06502v1); [HF FireRedChat-pvad](https://huggingface.co/FireRedTeam/FireRedChat-pvad/commit/f3712c517180dac84ac449f3dc8db765d665d5c1)). Its size, license and handling of the system's own TTS echo are not stated. Cheap CPU speaker encoders are mature: CAM++ (7.2M params, 0.65% VoxCeleb1-O EER) ([arXiv 2403.19971](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.19971)), WeSpeaker ResNet34 (26.5 MB ONNX, 0.723% EER) ([WeSpeaker](https://github.com/wenet-e2e/wespeaker/)), and SpeechBrain ECAPA (0.80%, Apache-2.0) ([HF](https://huggingface.co/speechbrain/spkrec-ecapa-voxceleb)). Generic anti-spoofing is a poor substitute: AASIST's 0.83% EER on ASVspoof 2019 becomes 43.01% on in-the-wild audio ([HF AASIST card](https://huggingface.co/SpeechAntiSpoofingBenchmarks/AASIST)).

**Inference.** Voxa should "anti-enroll" its own voice. Embedding Voxa's TTS output, both clean and re-recorded through the speaker, gives a free negative centroid. That turns "is this my own voice?" into a two-class closed-set decision (user centroid minus TTS centroid above a margin), which is far easier than open-set spoof detection. Run it only while TTS plays, so a cold, a whisper or a second household member costs nothing when Voxa is silent. The risks are real: speaker verification degrades with emotion, illness and distance ([arXiv 2412.00319](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.00319v1); [UEF](https://erepo.uef.fi/bitstream/handle/123456789/7740/1567597761506397310.pdf)), and errs more when voices are similar ([arXiv 2204.03793](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.03793v3)). Segment-level scores over 1–1.5 s with hysteresis are needed; 10 ms frame decisions are too noisy. Voiceprints are biometric data and must stay local and deletable.

Text is the third lever, and Voxa's current sentence-level filter leaves accuracy on the table. Whisper's native timestamps are unreliable at word level, which is why WhisperX adds CTC forced alignment ([arXiv 2303.00747](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.00747v2)), and Montreal Forced Aligner beats both on boundary accuracy ([arXiv 2406.19363](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2406.19363)). **Inference.** Voxa does not need to align its own side at all: Edge TTS already returns word boundaries (**Observed in Voxa**, `speech.py`), and Piper and Kokoro expose phoneme durations. A word-timed filter compares each faster-whisper word (with `word_timestamps=True`) against TTS words *played* within about ±300–500 ms of the same moment, after converting synthesis offsets to playback time. It drops only time-matched words. That handles partial echoes, and it keeps a user who interrupts with different words even mid-sentence. **Observed in Voxa:** `ownspeech.py` currently keeps 6 s of spoken text and fuzzy-matches words without timing, with protected words; `bargein.py` has a level-ratio `BargeInGate` (ratio 2.0, min level 0.02, 4 of the last N ticks).

A fourth, speculative lever is watermarking. AudioSeal (MIT, ICML 2024) gives per-sample localized detection and a causal streaming generator and detector with 320-sample frames ([arXiv 2401.17264](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.17264); [GitHub](https://github.com/facebookresearch/audioseal)). Its survival through a loudspeaker, a room and a mic is undocumented, so it belongs at the end of the roadmap as a bench experiment.

## Duck in 200 ms, commit in 800: turn-taking that never interrupts itself

Production voice-agent frameworks converge on three filters for interruptions: minimum speech duration, minimum transcribed words, and a backchannel list. They pair these with false-interruption recovery. LiveKit's defaults are `min_duration` 0.5 s, `false_interruption_timeout` 2.0 s and `resume_false_interruption=True`, so speech paused by a false interruption resumes ([LiveKit tuning](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/logic/turns/tuning/)). Vapi's `stopSpeakingPlan` suggests `numWords` 2–3, `voiceSeconds` about 0.2, `backoffSeconds` 1 s, plus ignored `acknowledgementPhrases` and instant `interruptionPhrases` ([Vapi](https://docs.vapi.ai/customization/voice-pipeline-configuration)). Pipecat applies `min_words` only while the bot is speaking ([Pipecat strategies](https://docs.pipecat.ai/server/utilities/turn-management/user-turn-strategies)). LiveKit's learned interruption model, which is cloud-only, shows what a learned onset classifier buys. It reports **86% precision and 100% recall at 500 ms of overlap, rejects 51% of VAD-triggered barge-ins, and needs a median of 216 ms of audio** ([LiveKit blog](https://livekit.com/blog/adaptive-interruption-handling)). Bad resumption is its own failure mode: Retell users report agents replaying an utterance from the start or speaking the remainder as a separate turn ([Retell community](https://community.retellai.com/t/issue-fragmented-agent-speech-partial-utterance-plays-then-restarts-from-the-beginning/2498.md)).

End-to-end full-duplex models do not solve this for Voxa. In Full-Duplex-Bench v1, dGSLM and Moshi take the floor during user pauses 93–98% of the time. Freeze-Omni does far better thanks to an explicit speak/listen state predictor, and the authors recommend such a module ([Full-Duplex-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04721v3)). Moshi-class 7B models also need a GPU. Voice Activity Projection (MIT, 50 Hz, two-channel input, real-time on CPU) is the research-grade predictive turn-taking model ([VAP repo](https://github.com/ErikEkstedt/VoiceActivityProjection); [arXiv 2401.04868](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04868v1)). Its one public checkpoint is trained on English telephone corpora, so it is an experiment for later.

Endpointing has moved from silence timers to "is the turn complete?" models. Google's unified ASR plus endpointer cut median endpoint latency by 120 ms and P90 by 170 ms with no WER loss ([arXiv 2211.00786](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.00786)). Amazon uses a two-pass endpoint arbitrator and per-user bandit configurations ([two-pass PDF](https://cdn.amazon.science/4b/7c/0903caee4ab2a19c5f185b0a5c1c/two-pass-endpoint-detection-for-speech-recognition.pdf); [bandits PDF](https://cdn.amazon.science/bb/e4/a2eb7078430095b60d1e8af610f2/adaptive-endpointing-with-deep-contextual-multi-armed-bandits.pdf)). OpenAI's `semantic_vad` caps waits at 8, 4 or 2 s by eagerness ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/docs/api-reference/realtime-sessions)). For local use, **Pipecat Smart Turn v3.1 is the best fit: BSD-2, an 8 MB int8 ONNX model on a Whisper-Tiny encoder, 94.7% English accuracy on its own test set, and 9–57 ms CPU inference plus 7–9 ms preprocessing**. It is meant to run on the whole turn audio after a short VAD silence, re-running if the user resumes ([Daily v3.1 blog](https://www.daily.co/blog/improved-accuracy-in-smart-turn-v3-1/); [smart-turn](https://github.com/pipecat-ai/smart-turn)). An independent five-conversation test measured 24.3% false positives ([soniqo.audio](https://soniqo.audio/blog/voice-agent-benchmarks)), so a minimum silence floor should stay. Pipecat pairs a 0.2 s VAD stop with the analyzer and a 5 s hard stop; LiveKit uses 0.3–2.5 s delays with its audio detector ([LiveKit turn detector](https://docs.livekit.io/agents/build/turns/turn-detector/)).

Losing words at the edges is a separate, cheap-to-fix problem. Silero's defaults are `speech_pad_ms=30` and `min_silence_duration_ms=100` on 32 ms windows ([silero-vad utils_vad.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/snakers4/silero-vad/master/src/silero_vad/utils_vad.py)), while OpenAI pads 300 ms before detected speech ([OpenAI VAD](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/realtime-vad)). For streaming partials, whisper_streaming's LocalAgreement-2 works with faster-whisper, but its README now calls it outdated in favour of SimulStreaming ([ufal/whisper_streaming](https://github.com/ufal/whisper_streaming)). Moonshine v2 Tiny Streaming reports 69 ms latency on Linux x86 at 12% WER ([arXiv 2602.12241](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.12241)).

**Inference.** Voxa's barge-in should be a three-state machine. **Suspect**: echo-aware VAD onset of 150–250 ms that the predicted echo cannot explain. Voxa then ducks TTS by 10–15 dB, keeps the playback sample pointer, and starts buffering mic audio with pre-roll. **Confirm**: within 0.4–1.0 s, two or more non-backchannel words, any protected command word, or at least 0.5 s of continuous unexplained speech hard-stops TTS, cancels the LLM, and keeps the buffered audio as the start of the user's turn. **Reject**: backchannels only, no words, bracketed non-speech tokens, or low `avg_logprob` restore volume or resume from the paused sample, rewinding to the last word boundary. Ducking rather than pausing keeps false positives inaudible. Two details are essential. Ducking changes the echo level, so the suspect detector must work on AEC output against the reference, not on raw levels; otherwise it oscillates. And Voxa must log the playback position at barge-in and truncate the assistant turn in the LLM context to what was actually heard, as OpenAI and Alexa do. Final ASR should always run once on the whole turn with at least 300 ms pre-roll from a 1 s ring buffer. Streaming partials are used only to count barge-in words.

## On Linux, play the TTS through the reference path instead of capturing the monitor

PipeWire's `libpipewire-module-echo-cancel` creates capture, source, sink and playback streams and runs the WebRTC backend in 10 ms blocks. It aligns mic and reference only by graph cycle plus an optional fixed `buffer.play_delay`, and its source "contains no explicit clock drift compensation" ([module-echo-cancel.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PipeWire/pipewire/master/src/modules/module-echo-cancel.c)). Its backend sets a fixed stream delay from the block count rather than a measured one ([aec-webrtc.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PipeWire/pipewire/master/spa/plugins/aec/aec-webrtc.cpp)). Users report it "only works if Microphone and Speakers are on the same device" ([pipewire#2165](https://gitlab.freedesktop.org/pipewire/pipewire/-/issues/2165)), and some versions ignored configured targets ([pipewire#2939](https://gitlab.freedesktop.org/pipewire/pipewire/-/issues/2939)). `monitor.mode` cancels everything on the default sink ([PipeWire docs](https://docs.pipewire.org/page_module_echo_cancel.html)). Inside a flatpak the PipeWire socket is not exposed; apps are expected to use `--socket=pulseaudio` ([PipeWire portal docs](https://docs.pipewire.org/page_portal.html); [WebKit bug 239682](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=239682)). **Observed in Voxa:** `echo.py` exists partly to clean up PulseAudio echo-cancel devices left by versions up to 0.1.5 that hijacked the user's speakers. Loading system modules is therefore a road already travelled and rejected.

GStreamer's `webrtcdsp` plus `webrtcechoprobe` is the flatpak-friendly in-process route. The probe belongs on the far-end path *before the speaker sink*. It converts each playback buffer's PTS to running time and pads or drops samples so each 10 ms reference chunk matches the measured pipeline latency ([webrtcdsp docs](https://gstreamer.freedesktop.org/documentation/webrtcdsp/webrtcdsp.html); [gstwebrtcechoprobe.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GStreamer/gstreamer/main/subprojects/gst-plugins-bad/ext/webrtcdsp/gstwebrtcechoprobe.cpp)). It accepts only 8/16/32/48 kHz, and the probe must match the DSP rate. **Observed in Voxa:** `audio.py`'s "webrtc" mode feeds the probe from a `pulsesrc` capturing the default-sink *monitor* into a `fakesink`, not from the TTS playback branch. **Inference.** In that layout the probe's latency arithmetic assumes the buffers are about to be played when they have in fact already been played. The reference also contains every other app's audio. AEC3's delay estimator may still lock, but the bench should compare this against a probe placed in the TTS `playbin` path.

For an exact own-sample reference outside GStreamer, PipeWire exposes `pw_stream_get_time_n()`, whose `delay`, `queued` and `buffered` fields give the time until a queued sample is heard ([pw_time docs](https://docs.pipewire.org/structpw__time.html)). PulseAudio exposes `pa_stream_get_latency`, and `pa_stream_set_monitor_stream()` restricts a monitor to a single sink input ([PulseAudio stream.h](https://freedesktop.org/software/pulseaudio/doxygen/stream_8h.html)). Latency reports are unreliable while pavucontrol is open ([pipewire#3191](https://gitlab.freedesktop.org/pipewire/pipewire/-/issues/3191)). Bluetooth adds codec buffering and profile switching. WirePlumber auto-switches to the headset profile when an input stream opens unless `bluetooth.autoswitch-to-headset-profile` is false ([WirePlumber BT](https://pipewire.pages.freedesktop.org/wireplumber/daemon/configuration/bluetooth.html); [Arch wiki](https://wiki.archlinux.org/title/PipeWire)).

**Inference.** The ranking for Voxa is clear. First choice: generate TTS PCM once at the AEC rate (16 kHz matches faster-whisper), play it through Voxa's own stream, and feed the identical samples to AEC3, indexed by measured playback time. Use either a probe in the TTS playback pipeline or `pywebrtc-audio` with timestamps from the sink's latency query. Second choice: a monitor restricted to Voxa's own sink input with `pa_stream_set_monitor_stream`. Never load modules into the user's daemon. On every default-device or BT profile change, flush the reference history, recreate the AEC, re-measure latency and re-check for headphones. Headphones and headsets mean no acoustic echo path, so cancellation can relax and barge-in can be more sensitive. Prefer the laptop mic when output is A2DP so Voxa never triggers the HFP autoswitch. The notes give no sourced per-codec A2DP latency, so the bench should test a 300 ms delay case and confirm that AEC3's search range covers it.

## Measure echo with ASR-level gates, not ERLE alone

ERLE is exact and cheap but only valid for far-end single talk in a quiet room, and it **correlates with human echo ratings at only PCC 0.31** (PESQ 0.67) ([arXiv 2309.12553](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.12553)). The Microsoft AEC Challenge therefore scores far-end single talk (FST), near-end single talk (NST) and double talk (DT) separately. It scores FST on the second half of each clip and DT on the last third so convergence does not dominate, and adds ASR word accuracy as an equal-weight term ([arXiv 2309.12553](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.12553)). AECMOS takes mic, loopback and output and tracks human ratings at PCC 0.99 for FST and DT echo ([arXiv 2110.03010](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.03010); [arXiv 2309.12553](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.12553)). It is installable as `speechmos`, an archived package that pins older dependencies ([PyPI speechmos](https://pypi.org/project/speechmos/)). Telephony standards give hard dB targets. ITU-T P.340 Category 1 "full duplex", as applied in ETSI TS 103 739, requires **at most 3 dB attenuation of near-end speech in double talk and at least 27 dB echo loss** ([ETSI TS 103 739](https://www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_TS/103700_103799/103739/01.01.02_60/ts_103739v010102p.pdf)). The challenge's synthetic recipe is directly reusable: RIRs with RT60 0.2–1.2 s, a loudspeaker nonlinearity on 80% of clips, SER uniform in −10 to +10 dB, and SNR 0–40 dB ([arXiv 2309.12553](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.12553)). The real recordings live in the challenge repo ([microsoft/AEC-Challenge](https://github.com/microsoft/AEC-Challenge)).

No standard defines the metrics Voxa actually cares about, so the bench must define them. Industry reports wake words as false accepts per hour at a fixed false-reject point ([Picovoice](https://picovoice.ai/blog/benchmarking-a-wake-word-detection-engine/)) and endpointers as EP50/EP90 latency ([arXiv 2011.10798](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.10798)). Barge-in has no standard beyond FireRedChat's false-barge-in rate and T90 ([arXiv 2509.06502](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.06502v1)) and vendor definitions of false stops ([Deepgram](https://deepgram.com/blog/elevenlabs-barge-in-interruptions-turn-taking); [Hamming](https://hamming.ai/blog/voice-agent-interruption-handling-runbook)). A practitioner write-up identifies the reverberant TTS tail surviving AEC as the classic self-interruption failure ([Zimaspace](https://shop.zimaspace.com/blogs/tech-ai-hub/local-voice-assistant-interrupts-itself-reverberant-room)).

**Inference.** The proposed metric set, computed over `jiwer` word alignments against both the user reference and the TTS script, is below. Every gate is a proposal to be ratcheted from the first baseline, except the two P.340-derived dB targets.

| Metric | Definition | Proposed gate |
|---|---|---|
| ERLE, FST, converged, synthetic | 10·log10(Σd²/Σŝ²) on far-end-only frames after 2 s | median ≥ 25 dB, P10 ≥ 15 dB |
| Near-end attenuation in DT | user-speech level in DT output minus same in NST output | ≤ 3 dB ([P.340 Cat 1](https://www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_TS/103700_103799/103739/01.01.02_60/ts_103739v010102p.pdf)) |
| AECMOS echo (FST, DT) | `speechmos` st/dt | ≥ 4.3 |
| FST transcript rate | TTS-only clips producing ≥ 1 ASR word / clips | ≤ 1% |
| Echo-leak word rate | ASR words matching TTS words played within ±1.5 s / TTS words played | ≤ 0.1% |
| False barge-in (self-interrupt) | barge-in triggers with no user onset / hour of TTS | 0 on smoke; ≤ 0.5/h full; ≤ 1/h with TV |
| Missed barge-in | attempts (≥ 2 words, ≥ 0.5 s) not ducking TTS within 1.0 s / attempts | ≤ 2% at SER ≥ −5 dB; ≤ 10% at −10 dB |
| Barge-in latency | t(duck) − t(user onset) | P50 ≤ 300 ms, P90 ≤ 600 ms |
| User word recall, NST | hits / reference words | ≥ 97% |
| WER penalty of DT | WER_DT − WER_NST | ≤ +3 points at SER ≥ −5 dB |
| First-word truncation | first reference word deleted or substituted / utterances | ≤ 1% cold start, ≤ 3% barge-in |
| Premature cut-off | endpoint before last word end / utterances | ≤ 1% |
| Endpoint latency | t(endpoint) − t(last word end) | EP50 ≤ 700 ms, EP90 ≤ 1200 ms |
| Recovery after echo-path change | time until ERLE ≥ 15 dB again | ≤ 2 s |

Report each metric per stratum (SER bin, RT60, nonlinear yes/no, drift yes/no, cold start vs barge-in) with bootstrap 95% CIs, so a regression points to its cause. Run Whisper with `temperature=0` and `condition_on_previous_text=False`, and log `no_speech_prob`, to separate hallucination on residual noise from genuine echo leak. **Observed in Voxa:** the repo already has a real-recording lab (`tools/anc_lab.py`, which plays via `paplay` and records via `parec` into `.voxa-spec/out/anclab/`), an offline webrtcdsp runner (`tools/webrtc_lab.py`), a Silero lab (`tools/voice_detect_lab.py`) and a wake-word replay (`tools/wake_replay.py`). The bench should absorb and standardise these rather than start from nothing.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the goal. "Never hear yourself" is not mainly an echo-cancellation problem: published systems get 15–25 dB from the linear stage and win or lose on what happens to the 10–20 dB residual. Voxa's decisive advantage is information, not DSP. It knows the exact samples, the words and the word times of its own voice, and when each sample reached the speaker. The vendors approximate that knowledge with fingerprints, watermarks and learned reference channels; Voxa can simply look it up, provided the plumbing hands it a timestamped reference instead of a jittery mixed monitor. The second reframing is that speed and robustness need not trade off when the stop is reversible. A 200 ms duck followed by a word-confirmed commit can match GPT-4o's stop latency without inheriting its 93% response rate to background speech.

What remains genuinely unknown matters for the plan. Nobody has published AEC3 or LocalVQE numbers on laptop speakers playing a single known TTS voice. No source shows how well speaker embeddings reject a specific local TTS voice replayed over the air, whether AudioSeal survives that path, or what drift rates rhY's actual devices have. Each of these is cheap to measure once the bench exists, which is why the bench is task one and every later change must move a bench number to land.

### B1. Build a seeded synthetic clip renderer
Build: `voxa/bench/render.py` that writes, for each clip, the separate near-end speech `s`, echo `d`, noise `n`, the mic mix, the far-end reference (the exact TTS samples), and a JSON truth file. The truth file holds user word onsets and offsets, TTS word timings, SER/SNR, RT60, delay, drift and the nonlinearity used. Steps: (1) collect TTS sources by rendering about 200 typical Voxa replies with word timings through Voxa's own TTS, reusing `tools/voice_bench.py` output where possible; (2) collect user sources from LibriSpeech test-clean plus rhY's recorded command phrases from `tools/anc_lab.py`'s COMMANDS list; (3) echo path from synthetic exponential-decay RIRs (RT60 0.2–0.8 s), adding pyroomacoustics only if it installs cleanly; (4) impairments of hard clip at 0.5–0.9 FS or `tanh(g·x)` with g 1–4 on 80% of clips, bulk delay 0–300 ms, an optional 20–80 ms mid-clip step, and drift ±0–200 ppm by resampling the echo but not the reference; (5) mix at SER ∈ {−10, −5, 0, +5, +10} dB and SNR ∈ {∞, 30, 20, 10} dB; (6) scenarios FST, NST, DT (user onset at TTS start and at 0.2/0.5/0.8 of the TTS), cold start (user within 200 ms of TTS end), and echo-path change. Measure: render time and a hash manifest. Gate: two renders with seed 0 give byte-identical manifests; a unit test confirms realised SER is within ±0.5 dB of target and injected delay within ±1 sample; the 200-clip smoke set renders in under 2 minutes.

### B2. Build a file-driven runner that drives the real pipeline
Build: `voxa/bench/runner.py`, which pushes mic and reference WAVs through the same chunk path the live app uses (`audio.mix_chunk` with `ReferenceRing`, the selected canceller, `refmask`, `VoiceDetector`, `BargeInGate`, `OwnSpeech`, `endpoint.is_complete`, then faster-whisper) in faster-than-real-time 20 ms chunks. It logs timestamped events: VAD on/off, suspect, duck, barge-in confirm, TTS stop, endpoint, transcript with word timestamps and `no_speech_prob`. Steps: (1) factor any live-only glue into a pure function both paths call, without changing behaviour; (2) add an echo-mode switch (voxa, webrtc via the existing `tools/webrtc_lab.py` code, off); (3) emit an `events.jsonl` per clip; (4) pin faster-whisper to `temperature=0`, `condition_on_previous_text=False`, fixed beam. Measure: transcript agreement and speed. Gate: on clean NST clips the runner's transcripts equal a direct `WhisperModel.transcribe` of the same audio on ≥ 98% of clips; the smoke suite runs at ≤ 0.5x real time on rhY's CPU.

### B3. Implement the metrics and the gate report
Build: `voxa/bench/metrics.py` and `report.py` implementing every row of the evaluation table above. Normalise text (lower-case, strip punctuation, numbers to words) and align with `jiwer.process_words` against both the user reference and the TTS script. Steps: (1) ERLE_true and near-end attenuation from the separate `d` and `s` tracks; (2) FST transcript rate and echo-leak words with the ±1.5 s TTS window; (3) false barge-ins per hour of TTS, missed barge-ins, and duck/stop latency from events versus truth onsets; (4) recall, DT WER penalty, first-word truncation, premature cut-off, EP50/EP90; (5) per-stratum breakdown and 1,000-sample bootstrap 95% CIs; (6) `bench_report.json` with value, CI, threshold and pass/fail, and a non-zero exit on any fail; (7) optional AECMOS via `speechmos` in a separate venv if its pins conflict. Gate: unit tests on 10 hand-built toy cases (for example one deleted first word, one echo word inside the window, one trigger with no onset) give exactly the expected values.

### B4. Record the real-room corpus in bench format
Build: a `record-bench` command in `tools/anc_lab.py` that produces the same file layout as B1. It covers TTS-only takes at the existing VOLUMES (0.2/0.4/0.7/1.0), rhY reading the COMMANDS and about 100 short phrases over playing TTS, and a TV/music-interference take. The mic and the exact reference are captured at 48 kHz, with a close-talk headset as an optional second channel for onset truth. Steps: (1) script prompts with on-screen cues; (2) store the reference that was actually sent to `paplay`; (3) label user onsets from the headset channel, or from Silero on a near-end-only retake; (4) write a checksum manifest into the git-ignored `.voxa-spec/out/` tree. Gate: ≥ 30 minutes of TTS-only audio and ≥ 100 labelled barge-in takes; the cross-correlation delay between reference and mic within one take has a standard deviation ≤ 1 ms, which proves the corpus is self-consistent.

### B5. Record the baseline and the ratchet file
Build: `voxa/bench/gates.toml` holding target thresholds plus a `baseline.json` from running the full suite on the synthetic and real sets in all three current echo modes. Steps: (1) run, (2) commit the report summary into `docs/`, (3) set each gate to the stricter of the proposed target and "baseline minus nothing", so no later task can regress any metric. Gate: report exists for all three modes, and the smoke suite passes against the ratchet in today's default mode. This proves the bench is green before any change.

### 1. Feed the canceller the exact, timestamped TTS samples
Build: a TTS reference tap. Synthesise or decode TTS, resample once to 16 kHz, and play exactly those samples, through the `playbin` pipeline with an `appsink`/tee branch or through a dedicated `appsrc → pulsesink` pipeline. Steps: (1) keep a ring of played PCM keyed by stream sample index; (2) query the sink's latency (GStreamer latency query or `pa_stream_get_latency`) to map index to monotonic play time; (3) stamp mic chunks with capture time minus capture latency; (4) serve the reference window for each mic chunk from the ring; (5) keep the monitor reference as a fallback switch. Touches `speech.py` and `audio.py`. Measure: alignment error between predicted echo time and the cross-correlation peak on the B4 corpus, and ERLE with a second app playing music during TTS. Gate: alignment error P90 ≤ 2 ms; FST ERLE with concurrent music within 1 dB of ERLE without it (the monitor reference is expected to fail this); no ERLE regression versus baseline.

### 2. Make WebRTC AEC3 the default linear stage
Build: an `Aec3Canceller` in `anc.py` with the same `process(mic, ref)` interface as `EchoCanceller`, backed by `pywebrtc-audio` `EchoCanceller` (16 kHz, 10 ms frames, `stream_delay_ms` = 0). Also add a variant of the existing GStreamer route with `webrtcechoprobe` moved onto the TTS playback branch from task 1. Steps: (1) add the dependency to the flatpak manifest and requirements; (2) check whether `has_clock_drift` is exposed and enable it when mic and sink are different devices; (3) keep NLMS selectable; (4) run the bench A/B across NLMS, AEC3 in-process and GStreamer probe-on-playback, and set the default to the winner. Measure: ERLE, near-end attenuation, AECMOS, CPU share. Gate: synthetic FST converged ERLE median ≥ 25 dB and P10 ≥ 15 dB; real-corpus ERLE ≥ baseline + 6 dB; DT near-end attenuation ≤ 3 dB; ≤ 10% of one core.

### 3. Re-estimate delay at every utterance and survive drift
Build: in `anc.py`, a GCC-PHAT delay estimate over the first 0.5 s of each TTS utterance, used as the coarse offset passed to AEC3 or used to shift the reference ring. Log the estimate and track its slope across utterances as a ppm estimate. Steps: (1) implement GCC-PHAT with a 0–400 ms search; (2) apply hysteresis so the offset changes only on a > 1 ms stable shift; (3) if the slope exceeds 50 ppm, enable the drift flag or resample the reference by the estimated ratio; (4) add bench strata for ±50/100/200 ppm and 20–80 ms steps. Gate: delay estimate within ±1 ms of truth after 0.5 s of TTS; ERLE at ±100 ppm within 3 dB of the no-drift case; recovery to ERLE ≥ 15 dB within 2 s after a 50 ms step.

### 4. Drive the per-band residual mask from AEC3's output
Build: in `refmask.py`, run `ReferenceMask` on the AEC3 output instead of the raw mic. Use AEC3's output-versus-reference ratio as the predicted residual, and stay active during TTS plus a 300 ms tail. Steps: (1) feed the AEC output and the aligned reference; (2) use a per-band over-subtraction that rises when ERLE is low (cheap speakers, high volume); (3) protect bands where the output energy exceeds the predicted residual by ≥ 6 dB; (4) cap TTS output peaks at −3 dBFS in `speech.py` to limit speaker compression. Measure: FST transcript rate, echo-leak words, DT WER penalty, near-end attenuation. Gate: FST transcript rate ≤ 1%; echo-leak words ≤ 0.1% of TTS words; DT WER penalty ≤ 3 points at SER ≥ −5 dB; near-end attenuation still ≤ 3 dB.

### 5. Gate barge-in on echo-aware evidence, Amazon-style
Build: in `voicedetect.py`, run Silero on the post-AEC, post-mask signal. In `bargein.py`, replace the raw level ratio with a double-talk test that combines (a) Silero probability ≥ a raised threshold (0.6–0.7) while TTS plays, (b) output energy ≥ predicted residual + margin, and (c) low short-time coherence between output and reference, or a drop in running ERLE as in [US10586534B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10586534). Steps: (1) compute running ERLE and coherence per 32 ms; (2) require all three for ≥ 150 ms to raise "suspect"; (3) add a 300 ms hold so brief residual bursts are ignored; (4) freeze AEC adaptation while suspect. Measure: false barge-ins per hour, missed barge-ins per SER bin, suspect latency. Gate: 0 false barge-ins on the smoke suite and ≤ 0.5/h on the full suite (≤ 1/h with TV); missed barge-in ≤ 2% at SER ≥ −5 dB and ≤ 10% at −10 dB.

### 6. Never lose the first or last word
Build: a 1 s raw-mic ring buffer, and turn audio assembled from the first onset minus at least 300 ms to the confirmed endpoint. Run one final faster-whisper pass on the whole turn, never on concatenated VAD segments. Steps: (1) add the ring in the capture path; (2) prepend 300 ms pre-roll, or 500 ms if the bench shows plosive loss; (3) lower Silero's off-threshold (neg threshold ≈ threshold − 0.15) and add a 200 ms trailing pad; (4) for barge-ins, start the turn at the suspect onset minus pre-roll, using post-AEC audio. Touches `voicedetect.py` and `speech.py`. Gate: first-word truncation ≤ 1% on cold starts and ≤ 3% on barge-ins; premature cut-off ≤ 1%; NST recall ≥ 97%.

### 7. Filter own speech by word and time, not by sentence
Build: in `ownspeech.py`, a playback word timeline from Edge TTS word boundaries (already requested in `speech.py`) or Piper/Kokoro durations, shifted by the measured output latency from task 1. Compare it with faster-whisper `word_timestamps=True` output, and drop an ASR word only if it fuzzy-matches a TTS word played within ±400 ms. Steps: (1) record (word, play_start, play_end); (2) align heard words; (3) keep protected words (stop/pause/cancel/quiet/wait) unless the TTS was saying that exact word at that moment and the task-5 gate is closed; (4) sweep the window over 200/400/600 ms on the bench. Gate: echo-leak words at most half of the task-4 result; user word recall in DT drops by ≤ 0.5 point versus no filter; protected command words in barge-in clips are kept 100% of the time.

### 8. Duck on suspicion, commit on words, resume on false alarms
Build: a three-state machine in `bargein.py`. Suspect (from task 5) ducks TTS by 12 dB in `speech.py` and records the sample position. Confirm, on ≥ 2 non-backchannel words from streaming partials, any protected word, or ≥ 0.5 s of continuous gated speech, hard-stops TTS, cancels generation and starts the user turn. Reject, on backchannels ("mm-hm", "yeah", "okay", "right"), bracketed non-speech tokens, `no_speech_prob` > 0.6, or 1.5 s without words, restores volume. Steps: (1) implement the ducking volume ramp (20 ms); (2) on confirm, store the heard text up to the stop position from the word timeline and truncate the assistant turn in the conversation context to that text; (3) on a long pause-then-reject, rewind to the last word boundary; (4) add bench clips with coughs, laughs and backchannels. Gate: duck latency P50 ≤ 300 ms and P90 ≤ 600 ms; ≥ 95% of backchannel and cough clips end in resume; ≥ 95% of real interruptions end in stop within 1.0 s; truncated context equals the played words exactly on 100% of test clips.

### 9. Add semantic endpointing with Smart Turn
Build: in `endpoint.py`, after a 250 ms VAD silence, run Smart Turn v3.1 (8 MB ONNX, `OMP_NUM_THREADS=1`, own thread) on the whole turn audio, combined with the existing text `is_complete`. Complete means end after a 500 ms total silence floor. Incomplete, or text ending in a conjunction, filler or preposition, means wait up to 2.0 s, re-running on each new pause. Keep a hard cap of 3 s, or 5 s in dictation. Steps: (1) package the model in the flatpak; (2) time it on rhY's CPU; (3) add bench clips with mid-sentence pauses of 0.4–1.5 s. Gate: EP50 ≤ 700 ms and EP90 ≤ 1200 ms; premature cut-off ≤ 1% including the pause clips; Smart Turn ≤ 60 ms per call.

### 10. React to device, headphone and Bluetooth changes
Build: in `echo.py`/`audio.py`, a pulsectl `event_listen` (or `pa_context_subscribe`) watcher. On a default sink or source change or a BT profile change, it flushes the reference ring, recreates the AEC, re-measures latency and classifies the output as speakers, headphones/headset, or Bluetooth A2DP/HFP, using `device.bus`, `device.form_factor` and port type checked against `pw-dump` on rhY's hardware. Headphones relax the barge-in thresholds of task 5. A2DP widens the delay search to 400 ms and raises the residual margin. Voxa never switches profiles itself. Steps: (1) watcher plus classification unit tests on recorded `pactl` output; (2) bench strata with a 300 ms bulk delay and a mid-clip device switch (delay and RIR jump). Gate: ERLE back to ≥ 15 dB within 2 s of a simulated switch; ERLE at 300 ms delay within 3 dB of the 50 ms case; no false barge-in during the switch.

### 11. Reject Voxa's own voice by speaker anti-enrollment
Build: `voxa/speakercheck.py` using CAM++ or WeSpeaker ResNet34 ONNX on CPU. A user centroid comes from 3–6 utterances, recorded at two distances; a Voxa-TTS centroid comes from clean TTS plus B4 re-recorded echo. During TTS only, a confirm (task 8) additionally requires user score minus TTS score ≥ margin on the latest 1.5 s segment. Support several user centroids and keep everything local and deletable. Steps: (1) time the encoders on 1.5 s segments; (2) build the centroids; (3) sweep the margin on B4; (4) wire it in behind a setting, off by default until the gate passes. Gate: on the recorded corpus, TTS-only segments rejected ≥ 99% and user barge-in segments accepted ≥ 97%; ≤ 50 ms per segment; missed-barge-in metric not worse than task 8.

### 12. Try LocalVQE as a neural residual postfilter
Build: an optional backend in `refmask.py` calling `liblocalvqe.so` via ctypes on (AEC3 output or raw mic, reference) at 16 kHz with a 256-sample hop ([HF LocalVQE](https://huggingface.co/Trondtempia/LocalVQE)). Compare it raw-mic-only and post-AEC3. Steps: (1) build the library into the flatpak; (2) bench both placements; (3) measure CPU. Gate: adopt only if FST transcript rate and DT WER penalty both beat task 4 beyond the CI, near-end attenuation stays ≤ 3 dB, and CPU stays ≤ 25% of one core; otherwise record the numbers and leave it off.

### 13. Test whether a watermark survives the speaker-to-mic path
